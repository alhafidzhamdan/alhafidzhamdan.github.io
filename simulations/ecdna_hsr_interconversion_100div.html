Extrachromosomal DNA · ecDNA ↔ HSR interconversion

From puncta to a staining region

The same EGFR amplicon can live in two forms. As ecDNA — acentric circles that replicate and segregate at random, amplifying fast but unstably. Or reintegrated into a chromosome as a homogeneously staining region (HSR) — a tandem array that is faithfully inherited by both daughters, stable but far less able to change copy number. Circles can reintegrate, and an array can re-excise. We follow a population under selection for oncogene dosage across 100 divisions and watch the amplicon move between the two compartments.

Where the amplicon lives

mean oncogene dosage split into ecDNA (circles) vs HSR (chromosomal) · division 0 → 100

The hand-off: selection rewards total dosage, and ecDNA finds it first — random segregation lets lucky lineages stack up circles quickly. But those gains are unstable. Each integration event copies part of that dosage into a chromosomal HSR, where it is transmitted intact to both daughters and can no longer drift away — so under sustained selection the dosage tends to migrate from circles into the array, while re-excision keeps a residual, plastic ecDNA pool alive.

Stability versus plasticity

cell-to-cell heterogeneity (left) falls as dosage becomes chromosomal (right)

Reading it: while the amplicon is extrachromosomal, sister cells inherit wildly different copy numbers — heterogeneity is high. As dosage is locked into the HSR, every daughter inherits the same array and the population homogenises: the heterogeneity curve tracks the mirror image of the HSR share. ecDNA supplies the evolvability; the HSR remembers the result.
