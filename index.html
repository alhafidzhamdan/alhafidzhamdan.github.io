About

I am a Pathology Resident and ECAT SCREDS Academic Clinical Lecturer based in Edinburgh, UK. I hold a joint appointment between NHS Lothian and the University of Edinburgh, where I conduct post-doctoral research in the Pollard lab at the Centre for Regenerative Medicine, Institute for Regeneration and Repair.

I completed a Cancer Research UK-funded PhD in the Semple lab at the Institute of Genetics and Cancer, where I led the analysis of the largest glioblastoma whole-genome sequencing cohort assembled to date. Building on this foundation, my current research centres on somatic and cancer evolution, with a particular focus on how genomic instability — including aneuploidy, chromothripsis, and extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) — drives tumour initiation, progression, and recurrence.

I am especially interested in integrating spatial multi-omics at single nucleus resolution with detailed histomorphological annotation to map these processes in their native tissue context. By combining expertise in pathology with advanced genomic technologies, I aim to uncover mechanisms of genomic instability and translate these insights into clinically relevant tools for early detection, risk stratification, and precision therapies in brain tumours and other cancers of unmet need.

Open to new collaborations and prospective PhD students. Feel free to get in touch.

Research

We study somatic and cancer evolution by contextualising genomic instability within the native tissue environment. Combining histopathological expertise with advanced genomic and spatial technologies, our work focuses on three interconnected themes:

Mechanisms underlying genomic instability

We investigate how genomic instability arises, persists, and is selected for during tumour evolution. Particular emphasis is placed on extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), chromothripsis, and other structural variants, including their lineage-specific vulnerabilities (e.g., in neural progenitors) and interplay with DNA double-strand breaks, R-loops, and mitochondrial–nuclear crosstalk. Using a combination of genome-edited cell models, patient-derived samples, and large whole-genome sequencing datasets, we seek to uncover the rules governing ecDNA formation, amplification, and inheritance in different tumour types. For a broader overview, see our recent review on structural variants in tumourigenesis.

Morphology-guided spatial single nucleus multi-omics

We aim to develop and apply spatially resolved approaches that preserve and leverage tissue morphology and cellular context to study tumour evolution in situ. By integrating single-nucleus multi-omics (e.g., scWGS and scRNA-seq) with spatial information (e.g. Slide-Tags barcoding), we will map the spatial organisation of genomic instability, ecDNA-driven heterogeneity, and immune-microenvironmental niches within intact tumours and premalignant lesions. This “morphology-aware” framework will enable precise linking of molecular features to histopathological patterns and immune compartments, improving biological insight and diagnostic relevance.

Tracing and targeting genomic instability

We aim to translate mechanistic and spatial insights into clinically actionable tools. This includes the future development of modern liquid biopsy approaches, as well as the identification of synthetic-lethal vulnerabilities and novel therapeutic targets. Our work is focused on cancers of unmet need, particularly malignant brain tumours such as glioblastoma and medulloblastoma, as well as high-risk neuroblastoma, high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma, and others, with the ultimate goal of improving early detection, risk stratification, and precision treatment strategies.
